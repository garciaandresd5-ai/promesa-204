**What Acemoglu gets right about Puerto Rico without mentioning it once**

In March, Daron Acemoglu stood at a lectern at Boston College and made an argument that has been building in his work for years, one that his forthcoming book will lay out in full this August: that liberal democracy is not losing to its enemies so much as losing to itself. I was in the room, mostly trying to keep up.

His central argument was this: the decline of liberal democracy is not mainly the story of authoritarian strongmen or disinformation campaigns, though those matter. It is the story of a coalition that broke from within. Starting around the 1980s, the educated class gradually took over the political parties that had historically belonged to workers. Social democratic and center-left parties across the industrialized world, the ones built by trade unionists and blue-collar organizers, became by degrees the parties of the urban credentialed. And as that happened, those parties stopped speaking the language of the people they were supposed to represent, growing distant from working-class concerns not through malice but through the quieter and more durable process by which people who socialize together, marry within the same circles and inhabit the same institutions come to share a way of seeing the world that those outside those circles do not recognize as their own. The fracture was not a betrayal in the dramatic sense. It was a slow drift, and in some ways more damaging for being so.

He was talking about France, Germany, the United Kingdom, the United States. He never mentioned Puerto Rico. But sitting in that lecture hall, I kept thinking about the island, because Puerto Rico is not a footnote to this argument. It is one of its clearest illustrations, made sharper by the colonial structure that gives the credentialed class here a kind of leverage over workers that their counterparts on the mainland can only approximate.

The standard story about Puerto Rico's educated class is one of departure, that talented people leave for the mainland, take their credentials with them and the island loses. That story has some truth to it but obscures the more important dynamic. Puerto Rico has not lost its educated population at disproportionate rates. The share of college-educated residents has actually grown over the past two decades, and the people with degrees largely came back, or never left. The question worth asking is what frameworks they brought with them when they did.

What they brought, inevitably, were the conceptual vocabularies of mainland universities and graduate programs, built to analyze economies that look very different from Puerto Rico's. This is not a character flaw. It is what institutions do. A technocrat trained in the postindustrial American tradition encounters a labor reform and instinctively processes it through the logic of competitiveness: what does this do to hiring costs, to investment, to the fiscal plan? A worker in Caguas or Bayamon encounters the same reform and asks a different question entirely: what does this mean for my paycheck on Sunday? These are not merely different opinions about the same policy. They are different ways of experiencing what the policy is, and the gap between them is not something that better messaging from the educated class can close, because messaging presupposes a shared frame that does not yet exist.

Before going further it is worth saying plainly: liberal democracy, for all its failures, has a better record than its alternatives. Acemoglu's own research shows that countries that democratize grow roughly 20% faster in the following two decades than those that do not, and that democratic governance reliably improves public goods provision, health outcomes and the distribution of income. The argument here is not against liberal democracy. It is about what liberal democracy loses when its coalition fractures, and why that fracture is especially costly in a place like Puerto Rico.

The labor law history of the past decade makes this concrete. In 2017, Governor Rossello signed the Labor Transformation and Flexibility Act, stripping workers of overtime protections, extending probationary periods and eliminating Sunday premium pay. The reform was advanced by the PROMESA oversight board, a body of federally appointed technocrats whose mandate came from Washington and whose authority derived from a federal law rather than from Puerto Rican voters. Workers who lost those protections had no formal role in the process by which the fiscal rationale for the reform was assembled and endorsed.

Five years later, Governor Pierluisi signed Act 41, restoring many of those rights. Workers had navigated the democratic process and produced a result. The oversight board objected almost immediately, arguing the government had failed to file the required fiscal impact assessment under PROMESA's Section 204. A federal judge sided with the board and declared Act 41 void, reinstating the 2017 framework. Workers had won through the only legitimate channel available to them, and a federal court, deferring to the fiscal logic of an unelected board, reversed it.

What Acemoglu's framework helps clarify is why that sequence did not generate the kind of sustained, cross-sectoral pressure that might have changed the political calculus. Puerto Rico has serious economists, capable lawyers and a growing infrastructure of research institutions doing genuine work. CNE, the island's leading economics think tank, has argued plainly that PROMESA's architecture rests on the colonial premise that Puerto Ricans require outside technocrats to manage their own affairs, a critique that is pointed, well-documented and widely cited in policy circles. But a critique that circulates within policy circles and a political coalition that actually feels the stakes together are not the same thing, and Acemoglu's diagnosis helps explain the distance between them: when the educated class and workers no longer share a language, the arguments that resonate in one world do not automatically produce pressure in the other.

Private sector union density in Puerto Rico sits below 2%, against roughly 6% on the mainland, itself already a diminished number by historical standards. This did not happen because workers stopped caring. The island's 1952 constitution enshrined the right to organize, the right to strike and collective bargaining, provisions won at the constitutional convention by socialist delegates who understood that labor protections require institutional defense. That tradition eroded in part because the connective tissue between the educated class and the labor movement, the parties, the unions and the civic associations that once translated between those worlds, was dismantled during the same decades that credentialing became the dominant pathway into public life, leaving behind a policy class fluent in fiscal analysis and largely disconnected from the organizational life of workers.

Acemoglu ended his lecture by calling for what he terms working-class liberalism, a politics that prioritizes jobs and shared prosperity while reconnecting with the actual cultural and economic language of working-class communities, not as a messaging strategy but as a genuine reorientation of who the coalition is built around and whose framework shapes the agenda. He acknowledged he did not have a clean answer for how to get there, and neither do I, but the first step is probably honesty about the nature of the problem: that the gap is structural, produced by specific institutional choices over several decades, and that closing it requires more than better communication from people who already have platforms.

Puerto Rico does not need a more sophisticated technocracy. It needs the kind of politics where the people who produce the analysis and the people most affected by it are required to reason together, in the same rooms, toward conclusions that neither could reach alone, where the educated class does not arrive with frameworks already assembled and workers do not encounter policy as something that happened to them. Until that becomes the norm, the educated class will keep doing serious work in a register that workers experience as someone else's conversation about their lives, and the political consequences of that distance, as Acemoglu would be the first to tell you, have a way of compounding.
