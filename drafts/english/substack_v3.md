**What Acemoglu gets right about Puerto Rico without mentioning it once**

In March, Daron Acemoglu gave a lecture at Boston College. Acemoglu is a Nobel laureate in economics, one of the most cited in the field, and he was there to preview arguments from his forthcoming book on the crisis of liberal democracy. The room was full of political scientists, philosophers and law professors. I was there too, mostly trying to keep up.

His central argument was this: liberal democracy's decline is not mainly the story of authoritarian strongmen or disinformation campaigns, though those matter. It is the story of a coalition that broke from within. Starting around the 1980s, the educated class took over the political parties that had historically belonged to workers. Social democratic and center-left parties across the industrialized world, the ones built by trade unionists and blue-collar organizers, became by degrees the parties of the urban credentialed. And as that happened, Acemoglu argued, those parties stopped speaking the language of the people they were supposed to represent. They became, in his words, oblivious to the problems of working-class people with lower education working in manual or service jobs. The fracture was not a betrayal in the dramatic sense. It was quieter than that, and in some ways more damaging: a slow drift into a different way of seeing the world, shaped by institutions that workers never entered.

He was talking about France, Germany, the United Kingdom, the United States. He never mentioned Puerto Rico. But sitting in that lecture hall, I kept thinking about the island, because Puerto Rico is not a footnote to this argument. It is one of its clearest illustrations, made sharper by the colonial structure that gives the credentialed class here a kind of leverage over workers that their counterparts on the mainland can only approximate.

The standard story about Puerto Rico's educated class is one of departure: talented people leave for the mainland, take their credentials with them and the island loses. That story has some truth to it but obscures the more important dynamic. Puerto Rico has not lost its educated population at disproportionate rates. The share of college-educated residents has grown. The people with degrees came back, or never left. The question worth asking is what happened when they returned, and what frameworks they brought with them.

What they brought, inevitably, were the conceptual vocabularies of mainland universities and graduate programs, built to analyze economies that look nothing like Puerto Rico's. This is not a character flaw. It is what institutions do. A technocrat trained in the postindustrial American tradition reads a phrase like labor market flexibility and processes it as a variable in a competitiveness equation, something to be optimized. A worker in Caguas or Bayamón reads the same phrase and thinks about the Sunday overtime premium they used to receive and no longer do. These are not different opinions about the same reality. They are different realities, and no amount of good intentions bridges that kind of gap from the top down.

The labor law history of the past decade makes this concrete. In 2017, Governor Rossello signed the Labor Transformation and Flexibility Act, stripping workers of overtime protections, extending probationary periods and eliminating Sunday premium pay. The reform arrived wrapped in the language that credentialed policy circles find persuasive: fiscal sustainability, structural adjustment, competitiveness. The PROMESA oversight board, a body of federally appointed technocrats accountable to no Puerto Rican voter, had pushed for it. The workers who lost those protections had no meaningful place in the rooms where the justifications were assembled.

Five years later, Governor Pierluisi signed Act 41, restoring many of those rights. Workers had navigated the democratic process and produced a result. The oversight board objected almost immediately, arguing the government had failed to file the required fiscal impact assessment under PROMESA. A federal judge sided with the board and declared Act 41 void. The 2017 framework was reinstated. Workers had won through the only legitimate channel available to them, and a federal court, deferring to the fiscal logic of an unelected board, reversed it.

What Acemoglu's framework helps clarify is why that sequence did not produce the sustained, coalition-level pressure that might have changed the outcome. Puerto Rico has serious economists, capable lawyers and a growing infrastructure of research institutions doing genuine work. CNE, the island's leading economics think tank, has argued plainly that PROMESA's architecture rests on the colonial premise that Puerto Ricans require outside technocrats to manage their own affairs. That argument exists. It circulates. It does not appear to have moved the needle, and the reason, I think, is the one Acemoglu identified in that lecture hall: when the educated class and workers no longer share a language, the arguments that resonate in policy circles do not automatically produce the political pressure that comes from a coalition that actually feels the stakes together.

Private sector union density in Puerto Rico sits below 2%, against roughly 6% on the mainland, itself already a diminished number. This did not happen because workers stopped caring. The island's 1952 constitution enshrined the right to organize, the right to strike and collective bargaining, provisions won at the constitutional convention by socialist delegates who understood that labor protections require institutional defense. That tradition eroded in part because the connective tissue between the educated class and the labor movement, the parties, the unions, the civic associations that once translated between those worlds, was dismantled during the same decades that credentialing became the dominant pathway into public life. What replaced it was a policy class fluent in fiscal analysis and largely disconnected from the organizational life of workers.

Acemoglu ended his lecture by calling for what he terms working-class liberalism, a politics that prioritizes jobs and shared prosperity while reconnecting with the actual cultural and economic language of working-class communities, not as a messaging strategy but as a genuine reorientation of who the coalition is built around and whose framework shapes the agenda. He acknowledged he did not have a clean answer for how to get there. Neither do I. But the first step is probably being honest that the gap is structural, not incidental, that it was produced by specific institutional choices over several decades, and that closing it requires more than better communication from people who already have platforms.

Puerto Rico does not need a more sophisticated technocracy. It needs the kind of politics where the people who produce the analysis and the people most affected by it are required to reason together, in the same rooms, toward conclusions that neither could reach alone. Until that becomes the norm, the educated class will keep doing serious work in a register that workers experience as someone else's conversation about their lives. And the political consequences of that distance, as Acemoglu would be the first to tell you, have a way of compounding.
