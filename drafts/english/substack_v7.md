**What Acemoglu gets right about Puerto Rico without mentioning it once**

In March, I attended a lecture at Boston College given by Nobel Laureate Daron Acemoglu, where he presented an evolving thesis that will be fully detailed in his upcoming book this August. His core premise is that the true threat to liberal democracy is internal rather than external. As I sat among philosophers, law professors, and political scientists, trying to keep up with the conversation, I found myself thinking about Puerto Rico in a way I had not quite articulated before.

His central argument was this: the decline of liberal democracy is not mainly the story of authoritarian strongmen or disinformation campaigns, though those matter. It is the story of a coalition that broke from within. Starting around the 1980s, the educated class gradually took over the political parties that had historically belonged to workers. Social democratic and center-left parties across the industrialized world, the ones built by trade unionists and blue-collar organizers, became by degrees the parties of the urban educated class. And as that happened, those parties stopped speaking the language of the people they were supposed to represent, growing distant from working-class concerns not through malice but through the quieter and more durable process by which people who socialize together, marry within the same circles, and inhabit the same institutions come to share a way of seeing the world that those outside those circles do not recognize as their own. The fracture was not a betrayal in the dramatic sense. It was a slow drift, and in some ways more damaging for being so.

Before going further, it is worth saying plainly that liberal democracy, for all its failures, has a better record than its alternatives. Acemoglu's own research shows that countries that democratize achieve roughly 20% higher GDP per capita in the long run than those that do not, and that democratic governance reliably improves public goods provision, health outcomes, and the distribution of income. This is not a piece against liberal democracy. It is a piece about what happens when you try to apply Acemoglu's framework to a place like Puerto Rico and find that it only gets you so far.

The initial application is convincing enough. Puerto Rico has not lost its educated population at disproportionate rates. The share of college-educated residents has actually grown over the past two decades, and the people with degrees largely came back, or never left. What they brought with them, inevitably, were the conceptual vocabularies of mainland universities and graduate programs, built to analyze economies that look very different from Puerto Rico's. This tendency is not a character flaw. It is what institutions do everywhere. A technocrat trained in the postindustrial American tradition encounters a labor reform and instinctively processes it through the lens of competitiveness: what does this do to hiring costs, to investment, to the fiscal plan? A worker in Caguas or Bayamon encounters the same reform and asks a different question entirely: what does this mean for my paycheck? On the mainland, stronger unions and labor parties have historically translated that question into political pressure. In Puerto Rico, the labor movement was never institutionally strong enough to perform that translation at scale, and the 1952 constitution's guarantees of the right to organize and strike eroded alongside the civic associations and party structures that once gave them force.

The labor law history of the past decade seems, at first, to confirm the Acemoglu diagnosis. In 2017, Governor Rossello signed the Labor Transformation and Flexibility Act, stripping workers of overtime protections, extending probationary periods, and eliminating Sunday premium pay. Rossello was democratically elected, which means workers bore some indirect responsibility for the law's passage, but the reform was advanced and endorsed by the PROMESA oversight board, a body of federally appointed technocrats whose mandate came from Washington and whose authority derived from federal law rather than from Puerto Rican voters. Five years later, Governor Pierluisi signed Act 41, restoring many of those rights. The oversight board objected almost immediately, arguing the government had failed to file the required fiscal impact assessment under PROMESA's Section 204, and a federal judge sided with the board and declared Act 41 void. Workers had won through the only legitimate channel available to them, and a federal court, deferring to the fiscal logic of an unelected board, reversed it.

This is where the Acemoglu framework starts to show its limits, and where the more interesting question begins. The standard reading of that sequence would be that the policy class failed to generate enough pressure to defend the workers' victory, the kind of coalition failure Acemoglu describes. But that reading does not hold up cleanly when you look at what the Puerto Rican policy class was actually doing. CNE, the island's leading economics think tank, has argued plainly and repeatedly that PROMESA's architecture rests on the colonial premise that Puerto Ricans require outside technocrats to manage their own affairs. Espacios Abiertos, the Centro de Periodismo Investigativo, and significant parts of the academic community have been consistently vocal against the board's interventions. The policy class was not asleep. The coalition was not obviously fractured along the lines Acemoglu describes.

So if the domestic policy class and workers were broadly aligned in opposing the board's intervention, why did Act 41 still fail? The answer points to something Acemoglu's framework was not built to account for. Puerto Rico has most of the machinery of a liberal democracy: free elections, an independent judiciary, civil liberties, a free press, and competitive political parties. But it operates within a federal structure that contains a specific and consequential carve-out, namely that on fiscal and labor policy, the decisions of its democratically elected government can be nullified by a board whose members Puerto Rican voters did not elect and cannot remove. That is not coalition fracture. That is a structural exception to democratic accountability built into the governing law of the territory.

Some would argue this is not so different from constraints that exist in any democracy. The Federal Reserve is not elected. The Supreme Court strikes down legislation. International bodies impose conditions on sovereign governments. But those institutions constrain democracies from within systems where the people affected have full political standing, with senators, voting representatives, and constitutional recourse. Puerto Rico had none of those when Congress passed PROMESA in 2016. The constraint is not internal to a democratic system Puerto Rico fully belongs to. It is external to one it only partially inhabits, and that distinction matters for what kind of problem the Act 41 reversal actually represents.

Acemoglu's working-class liberalism calls for rebuilding the coalition between the educated class and workers, reconnecting the language of policy to the lived experience of the people policy affects, and prioritizing jobs and shared prosperity over the cultural agenda of the credentialed. That prescription makes sense for France, for the United Kingdom, for the United States, where the problem is internal drift and the tools of democratic accountability are, in principle, available to any coalition strong enough to use them. For Puerto Rico, the prescription arrives at a different wall. The problem is not primarily that the coalition is fractured. It is that even a relatively intact coalition, one where the policy class and workers are broadly aligned, runs into a governing structure that can override their shared democratic will on the questions that matter most.

That is the limit the piece kept running into as I tried to make the Acemoglu argument work for the island. His framework is a diagnosis of liberal democracies fracturing from within. Puerto Rico's situation is different: it is a place with most of the features of a liberal democracy but without the full democratic standing that would make the standard remedies available. Rebuilding the coalition is necessary but not sufficient. The deeper question, which Acemoglu's framework does not quite reach, is what working-class liberalism looks like in a territory that has never had the democratic sovereignty to fully practice it.
