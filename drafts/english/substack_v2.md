**What Acemoglu gets right about Puerto Rico without mentioning it once**

Daron Acemoglu's forthcoming book, What Happened to Liberal Democracy?, arrives this August with an argument that has been building quietly in his work for years: that liberalism lost its way not because its enemies grew stronger, but because its coalition fractured from within. The center-left, which spent a century as the political home of the working class, became over the course of the postindustrial economy the party of the urban educated, and in making that transition it stopped speaking a language that workers recognized as their own. Trade unionists and blue-collar organizers, who once led the movement, gave way to credentialed professionals whose politics were shaped by institutions that workers never entered. The fracture, Acemoglu argues, is not incidental to liberalism's crisis. It is the crisis.

He is writing about the industrialized world in the aggregate. He never mentions Puerto Rico. But the argument lands on the island with a precision that feels almost deliberate, because Puerto Rico offers something the European and American cases do not: a compressed, almost laboratory-clean version of the same failure, made legible by the island's colonial structure and the peculiar way that structure intersects with credentialism.

The standard telling of Puerto Rico's educated class problem is a brain drain story: talented people leave for the mainland, take their expertise with them and leave the island worse off for it. The data complicates that narrative considerably. Puerto Rico has not lost its educated population at disproportionate rates; the island trains a large number of degree holders, many study on the mainland and return, and the share of college-educated residents has actually grown over the past two decades. The problem is not that the educated class abandoned Puerto Rico. The problem is what happened after they came back.

What came back with them, inevitably, were the frameworks. The conceptual vocabularies of mainland universities, graduate programs and policy institutions, built to analyze economies and labor markets that look nothing like Puerto Rico's. A technocrat trained in the postindustrial American tradition reads "labor market flexibility" as a variable in a competitiveness equation. A worker in Caguas or Ponce reads the same phrase and thinks about the Sunday overtime premium they are no longer owed. These are not simply different opinions about the same policy. They are different ways of perceiving what the policy is, different ontologies dressed up as a technical disagreement, and no amount of good intentions closes that kind of gap from the top down.

The labor law history of the past decade makes this visible with uncomfortable clarity. In January 2017, Governor Ricardo Rossello signed the Labor Transformation and Flexibility Act, stripping workers of overtime protections, extending probationary periods and eliminating Sunday premium pay. The reform was framed in the language that credentialed policy circles find persuasive: competitiveness, fiscal sustainability, structural reform. The PROMESA oversight board, composed of federally appointed technocrats accountable to no Puerto Rican voter, had pushed for it. Workers who lost those protections had no meaningful seat at the table where the justifications were assembled.

Five years later, in June 2022, Governor Pedro Pierluisi signed Act 41, restoring many of those rights. Workers had navigated the democratic process and produced a result. The oversight board objected almost immediately, arguing that the government had failed to submit the required fiscal impact assessment under PROMESA's Section 204. In March of that year, Judge Laura Taylor Swain sided with the board and declared Act 41 void, reinstating the 2017 framework. Workers had won through the only legitimate channel available to them. A federal court, deferring to the fiscal logic of an unelected board, took it back.

What is striking about that sequence is not the outcome itself, though the outcome is genuinely damaging. What is striking is how little sustained, visible pressure the island's credentialed policy class applied while it was happening. Puerto Rico has sophisticated economists, capable legal scholars, a growing infrastructure of research organizations doing serious work. CNE, the island's leading economics think tank, has been clear that PROMESA's architecture is built on the colonial premise that Puerto Ricans require outside technocrats to manage their own affairs. That argument exists in the literature. It circulates in policy circles. But it did not produce the kind of sustained, coalition-level pressure that might have changed the political calculus around the board's intervention in Act 41, and the absence of that pressure is worth taking seriously as a structural problem rather than a failure of individual will.

Private sector union density in Puerto Rico sits below 2%, against roughly 6% on the mainland, which is itself an already diminished number by historical standards. This is not an accident of culture or temperament. The island's 1952 constitution enshrined the right to organize, the right to strike and collective bargaining, rights won at the constitutional convention by socialist delegates who understood that labor protections required institutional defense. That tradition did not erode because workers stopped caring about it. It eroded in part because the institutional connective tissue between the educated class and the labor movement, the parties, the unions, the civic associations that once translated between those worlds, was dismantled during the same decades that credentialing became the dominant pathway into public life. What replaced it was a policy class fluent in fiscal analysis and largely unconnected to the organizational infrastructure of workers.

Acemoglu's framing is useful here precisely because it refuses the bad-faith interpretation. The educated elite did not set out to abandon workers. The coalition fractured because the credentialing process is not politically neutral. It moves people onto different timelines, installs different default assumptions, and makes certain kinds of arguments feel self-evidently reasonable while others require translation. By the time a graduate of a mainland economics program returns to Puerto Rico, the gap is not a matter of caring more or less. It is a matter of perceiving the problem differently at the level of first principles, and that kind of gap does not close through individual goodwill. It closes through institutional design, through the deliberate construction of spaces where the educated class and workers are required to reason together toward shared conclusions.

That is the piece Puerto Rico's policy conversation has been missing. Not more sophisticated analysis of the fiscal plan, though that matters. Not more technically precise arguments about labor market efficiency, though those have their place. What has been missing is the institutional architecture that would force the people who produce that analysis into sustained, accountable relationships with the people most affected by it, so that the frameworks themselves get pressure-tested against realities they were not built to describe. Until that happens, the educated class will continue doing serious work in a register that workers experience as someone else's conversation about their lives, and the political consequences of that distance will keep compounding in ways that no amount of competent technocracy will fix.
