**What Acemoglu gets right about Puerto Rico without mentioning it once**

In March, I attended a lecture at Boston College given by Nobel Laureate Daron Acemoglu, where he presented an evolving thesis that will be fully detailed in his upcoming book this August. His core premise is that the true threat to liberal democracy is internal rather than external. As I sat among philosophers, law professors, and political scientists, trying to keep up with the conversation, I realized his argument about the self-inflicted decline of democracy provides a framework for understanding the current state of Puerto Rico.

His central argument was this: the decline of liberal democracy is not mainly the story of authoritarian strongmen or disinformation campaigns as often described, though those matter. It is the story of a coalition that broke from within. Starting around the 1980s, research on party composition across industrialized democracies shows that the educated class gradually took over the political parties that had historically belonged to workers. Social democratic and center-left parties across the industrialized world, the ones built by trade unionists and blue-collar organizers, became by degrees the parties of the urban educated class. And as that happened, those parties stopped speaking the language of the people they were supposed to represent, growing distant from working-class concerns and solutions. Not through malice, but through the quieter and more durable process by which people who socialize together, marry within the same circles, and inhabit the same institutions come to share a way of seeing the world that those outside those circles do not recognize as their own. The fracture was not a betrayal in the dramatic sense. It was a slow drift, and in some ways more damaging for being so.

Before going further, it is worth saying plainly that liberal democracy, for all its failures, has a better record than its alternatives. Acemoglu's own research shows that countries that democratize achieve roughly 20% higher GDP per capita in the long run than those that do not, and that democratic governance reliably improves public goods provision, health outcomes, and the distribution of income. The argument here is not against liberal democracy. It is about what liberal democracies lose when their coalitions fracture, and why that fracture is especially costly in a place like Puerto Rico.

He was talking about France, Germany, the United Kingdom, the United States and never mentioned Puerto Rico. But sitting in that lecture hall, I kept thinking about the island, because Puerto Rico is not a footnote to this argument. Puerto Rico's case is different and in some ways more instructive: the island never built a strong labor-left coalition to fracture in the first place, and what Acemoglu describes as a slow drift away from workers happened here not through a party realignment but through the imposition of a colonial fiscal structure that placed the most consequential decisions beyond democratic reach entirely.

The standard story about Puerto Rico's educated class is one of migration where talented people leave for the mainland, take their credentials with them, and the island loses. That narrative has some truth to it but obscures the more important dynamic. Puerto Rico has not lost its educated population at disproportionate rates. The share of college-educated residents has actually grown over the past two decades, and the people with degrees largely came back, or never left. The question worth asking is what frameworks did they learn, and how did they apply them?

What they brought, inevitably, were the conceptual vocabularies of mainland universities and graduate programs, built to analyze economies that look very different from Puerto Rico's. This tendency is not unique to Puerto Rico, nor is it a character flaw. It is what institutions do everywhere. A technocrat trained in the postindustrial American tradition encounters a labor reform and instinctively processes it through the lens of competitiveness and elasticity: what does this do to hiring costs, to investment, to the overarching fiscal plan? A worker in Caguas or Bayamon encounters the same reform and asks a different question entirely: what does this mean for my paycheck? Mainland workers ask the same question too, but on the mainland there are stronger institutional channels, stronger unions, stronger labor parties that historically translated that question into political pressure. In Puerto Rico, under PROMESA, even when that pressure produces a result at the ballot box, it can be undone by a federal court. This disparity is not something that better messaging from above can close, because messaging presupposes a shared frame and shared institutional leverage that does not yet exist.

The labor law history of the past decade makes this concrete. In 2017, Governor Rossello signed the Labor Transformation and Flexibility Act, stripping workers of overtime protections, extending probationary periods, and eliminating Sunday premium pay. Rossello was democratically elected, which means workers bore some indirect responsibility for the law's passage. But the reform was advanced and endorsed by the PROMESA oversight board, a body of federally appointed technocrats whose mandate came from Washington and whose authority derived from a federal law rather than from Puerto Rican voters. The board's fiscal framework had already constrained what any elected governor could realistically do, which is precisely what makes PROMESA's architecture so consequential: it does not eliminate democratic choice, it narrows it until the choices that remain are ones the board can live with.

Five years later, Governor Pierluisi signed Act 41, restoring many of those rights. Workers had navigated the democratic process and produced a result. The oversight board objected almost immediately, arguing the government had failed to file the required fiscal impact assessment under PROMESA's Section 204. A federal judge sided with the board and declared Act 41 void, reinstating the 2017 framework. Workers had won through the only legitimate channel available to them, and a federal court, deferring to the fiscal logic of an unelected board, reversed it.

What Acemoglu's framework helps clarify is why that sequence did not generate the kind of sustained, cross-sectoral pressure that might have changed the political calculus. Puerto Rico has serious economists, capable lawyers and a growing infrastructure of research institutions doing genuine work. CNE, the island's leading economics think tank, has argued plainly that PROMESA's architecture rests on the colonial premise that Puerto Ricans require outside technocrats to manage their own affairs, a critique that is pointed, well-documented and widely cited in policy circles. But the problem is not that the domestic policy class is indifferent to workers. It is that the institutional architecture they all operate inside, PROMESA above all, was designed to insulate fiscal decisions from democratic accountability. Without the kind of broad labor coalition that never fully formed in Puerto Rico, there is no mechanism to generate the popular pressure that might force that architecture to change.

Private sector union density in Puerto Rico sits at around 3%, against roughly 6% on the mainland, itself already a diminished number by historical standards. This did not happen because workers stopped caring. The island's 1952 constitution enshrined the right to organize, the right to strike, and collective bargaining, provisions won at the constitutional convention by socialist delegates who understood that labor protections require institutional defense. That tradition eroded in part because the connective tissue between the policy class and the labor movement was dismantled during the same decades that credentialing became the dominant pathway into public life. The parties, the unions, the civic associations that once translated between those worlds gradually disappeared, leaving behind institutions fluent in fiscal analysis and largely disconnected from the organizational life of workers.

Acemoglu ended his lecture by calling for what he terms working-class liberalism, a politics that prioritizes jobs and shared prosperity while reconnecting with the actual cultural and economic language of working-class communities, not as a messaging strategy but as a genuine reorientation of who the coalition is built around and whose framework shapes the agenda. He acknowledged he did not have a clean answer for how to get there, but the first step is probably honesty about the nature of the problem: that the gap is structural, produced by specific institutional choices over several decades, and that closing it requires more than better communication from people who already have platforms.

Puerto Rico does not need a more sophisticated technocracy. It needs the kind of politics where the people who produce the analysis and the people most affected by it are required to reason together, in the same rooms, toward conclusions that neither could reach alone, where those with institutional power do not arrive with frameworks already assembled, and workers do not encounter policy as something that happened to them. Until that becomes the norm, the coalition that Puerto Rico needs to actually defend its labor institutions will keep failing to form, not because anyone willed it that way, but because the architecture makes it nearly impossible to build.
