What Acemoglu gets right about Puerto Rico without mentioning it once

The summer I interned in Caguas, I spent most of my days in a small office with a handful of people who all came from roughly the same place. Same island, same socioeconomic background, same general trajectory. The one thing that differed was where some of us had gone to school. And that difference, it turned out, was enough.

One of the interns had gone to Northeastern. The conversations I had with him moved differently than the ones I had with everyone else. Not smarter, not more informed about Puerto Rico specifically. But operating on a different clock. We were students talking about AI tools that the practitioners around us hadn't fully caught up to yet. The credential hadn't just taught him things. It had reorganized how he processed the present.

I didn't have a framework for that observation at the time. I do now.

Daron Acemoglu's forthcoming book, What Happened to Liberal Democracy?, releases this August. In it, the Nobel laureate argues that liberalism lost its way in the postindustrial economy when a segment of college-educated elites became politically dominant and separated from the rest of society. The center-left, he has said, used to be the party of the working class. Trade unionists and blue-collar workers led it. Now the urban educated lead it, and working-class voters have migrated toward the populist right. That fracture, Acemoglu argues, is not incidental to liberalism's crisis. It is central to it.

Acemoglu is writing about the industrialized world. He never mentions Puerto Rico. But the argument lands on the island with unusual force, and the reason is specific: Puerto Rico is not a case where the educated class left and abandoned workers. The educated class stayed. They are here, running think tanks and policy shops and government agencies. The disconnect is not geographic. It is epistemological.

Consider what happened to labor law on the island over the past decade. In January 2017, Governor Ricardo Rossello signed the Labor Transformation and Flexibility Act, which cut overtime pay, extended probationary periods and eliminated Sunday overtime premiums. The package was framed as a business-friendly reform designed to restore competitiveness. The PROMESA oversight board, staffed by federally appointed technocrats, had pushed for it. Workers who lost those protections did not get a vote.

Five years later, in June 2022, Governor Pedro Pierluisi signed Act 41, which restored many of those rights. Workers had, in effect, won through the democratic process. A month later, a federal judge declared Act 41 void. The oversight board had objected, and the court upheld its authority under PROMESA. The protections were stripped back. The 2017 framework was reinstated.

That sequence matters. Workers organized, elected officials who responded, passed legislation, and then watched a federal court reverse it on procedural grounds tied to a fiscal plan written by people none of them chose. The institutions built to manage Puerto Rico's debt crisis were, structurally, more powerful than the island's democratic will. And the people best positioned to argue loudly against that arrangement, the credentialed policy class with platforms and access and the right vocabulary, did not make enough noise.

This is not an argument about bad faith. I do not think Puerto Rico's educated class is indifferent to workers. I think the institutions that trained them, on the mainland, in graduate programs, in DC policy circles, handed them a set of lenses that do not translate cleanly back to the island. The same way a technocrat trained in neoliberal economics reads "labor market flexibility" as a competitiveness variable while a worker in Caguas reads it as a lost Sunday premium. Same policy. Different realities.

The data reinforces this. Private sector union density in Puerto Rico sits below 2%, compared to roughly 6% on the mainland, which is itself an already low number. The Puerto Rican labor movement was historically vibrant. The island's 1952 constitution included the right to organize, the right to strike and the right to collective bargaining, won by socialist delegates at the constitutional convention. That tradition did not collapse because workers stopped caring. It collapsed in part because the institutional infrastructure that used to connect educated organizers to workers, the parties, the unions, the civic associations, has been replaced by a credentialed policy class that speaks a language workers did not learn.

CNE, the island's leading economics think tank, has described PROMESA as premised on the colonial assumption that Puerto Ricans cannot govern themselves and therefore require a small group of technocrats to put the house in order. That framing is pointed, but the critique it contains is not limited to federal overseers. It applies with some force to the domestic policy class as well. The question is not only who holds formal power. It is whose conceptual framework shapes the terms of the debate.

Acemoglu's argument is ultimately about coordination failure. The educated elite did not set out to abandon the working class. The coalition fractured because the credentialing process moved one group onto a different timeline, with different tools, different frameworks, different ways of naming problems. By the time they returned, or stayed, the shared vocabulary that makes political coalitions possible had eroded.

Puerto Rico is a compressed version of that story. The island produces a large number of college graduates, many of whom study on the mainland and return. It is not losing its educated class at disproportionate rates. It is absorbing them back into institutions that are functionally disconnected from the workers those institutions are supposed to serve. The result is a policy environment where sophisticated fiscal analysis coexists with a labor movement that can win at the ballot box and still lose in federal court, and where the people best positioned to close that gap are operating in a register that the people most affected by it do not recognize as their own.

I was a student in Caguas watching another student talk about tools that practitioners had not yet learned. It felt like a gap in knowledge. It was actually a gap in time. That is the thing worth closing.
